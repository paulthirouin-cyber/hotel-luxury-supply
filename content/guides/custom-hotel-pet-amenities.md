# Custom Hotel Pet Amenities: Building a Hotel Pet Program

Pet-friendly used to mean a policy. At luxury properties it now means a program: a welcome at check-in, a bed and bowl already in the room, a walking map at the desk, a menu from the kitchen. What most programs still lack is a branded object. The bowl is from a pet store, the bandana is the sponsor's, and the one guest who photographs everything is photographing someone else's logo. This guide covers the six pieces HL Supply produces for a hotel pet program, which finish suits which use, how an in-room program differs from boutique retail, and how to write the brief so the collection arrives in time for the season.

## Why a pet program needs its own collection

The guest who travels with a dog is a high-value, high-loyalty guest who returns to properties that made the animal welcome, and who posts about it. A branded bowl in the room costs less than a single night's rate and is photographed more than the lobby. The economics are also unusual: there is no established hospitality supplier for pet amenities, so a property that commissions a collection defines the standard rather than matching one. The six pieces below were selected on three rules. Every item is one-size, so there is no inventory by breed. Nothing is clothing, because fit and comfort vary too much to brand responsibly. And every piece reuses a finishing method the atelier already runs for the rest of the catalog, so the pet collection can be matched exactly to the bottles, the leather suite set, and the totes.

## Etched steel, debossed leather, or printed textile: which finish for which use

The pet collection uses three finishing families, and the right one follows from where the piece lives.

## In-room program versus boutique retail

The two halves of a pet program have different jobs, and the brief should treat them separately even when the pieces overlap.

**The in-room program** is an amenity. It is set before arrival for a guest who declared a pet at booking, and it has to be presentable on the floor of a suite, washable, and easy for housekeeping to reset. The usual set is the Companion Bowl, the Harbor Rope Toy, and a Scout Bandana folded on the bed, with a card inviting the guest to keep the bandana and the toy. The bowl stays and is washed; the textile pieces are consumed at a predictable rate per pet stay, which is the number to brief. Properties that run a declared-pet rate of a few stays a week need a modest run of textile pieces and a single bowl inventory with spares.

**Boutique retail** is a product line. The Promenade Leash, the Beacon pouch, the Voyager Travel Bowl, and the Scout Bandana sit together as a small collection, priced and merchandised with the rest of the [retail collection](glossary:retail-collection), ideally beside the leather goods they match. Retail pieces need presentation packaging and a hangtag, both quoted alongside the goods. The practical advice is to launch both halves in one colorway so the guest who met the bowl in the room recognizes the lead in the shop.

Every pet piece is produced in any custom color, and the program works best when it is not specified in isolation. Match the bowl to the suite bottle, the leather pieces to the valet tray and luggage tag, and the bandana to the pool-deck stripe or the spa palette. Supply vector artwork and Pantone references with the brief; the atelier produces a mockup showing the mark at bowl, handle-wrap, pouch, bandana, and label scale, then a proof on the confirmed material. The etch on steel and the deboss on leather suit a wordmark or monogram; the print on the bandana can carry a full-color crest. Tone-on-tone treatments read as the most expensive and photograph the most calmly.

## Writing the brief

A complete brief for a pet program covers seven things, and a brief that covers them all is quoted in days rather than weeks.

1. **The two uses.** Which pieces are in-room, which are retail, and which are both.
2. **The pet stays.** Declared-pet stays per month, so textile consumption and bowl inventory can be sized.
3. **The pieces.** Any of the six catalog pieces, with quantities at or above the minimums: bowl, leash, and pouch from 100; bandana, travel bowl, and rope toy from 200.
4. **Colors.** Pantone references or the matching catalog pieces the program should sit beside.
5. **Artwork.** Vector logo files and a note on which mark goes where: wordmark on steel and leather, crest on textile.
6. **Packaging.** Whether retail pieces need presentation packaging and hangtags, and whether the in-room set needs a card.
7. **The date.** A season launch, an opening, or a boutique reset. Production is 4 to 6 weeks after proof approval, plus delivery; staged delivery suits programs that launch in-room first and retail second.

Put the pieces in the [quote cart](quote) with notes on each, and the concierge replies with direction, a mockup, and the quote. A piece the catalog does not include, a different material, or a program-specific finish is a request for the [Bespoke Atelier](bespoke).

## Common mistakes

- **Branding clothing.** Coats and sweaters need sizes and fit; a one-size bandana does the same job in photographs with none of the inventory.
- **Choosing a bowl by look alone.** A polished bowl without a non-slip base slides on stone and marks timber; housekeeping notices before the guest does.
- **Mixing colorways between room and shop.** The guest who met a navy bowl in the suite does not connect it to a terracotta lead in the boutique.
- **Skipping the proof on textile.** A print that is not colorfast fades on the first wash; approve the finish on the proof, not the mockup.
- **Under-ordering the take-home piece.** The bandana is consumed per pet stay and is the piece guests ask to buy; brief it at the higher end.
